# Track 3 — Scouting Existing Projects (Teardowns)

## 1. Orientation

This track's job is to know the genre cold before we design anything: what open-source (and closed) satellite/spacecraft visualization and mission-design tools already exist, what each one does well, where each breaks, and what is genuinely missing. It exists to make sure Phase 0/A (a satvis-parity demo on real ISS elements), Phase B (a heliocentric/interplanetary layer with Lambert/porkchop math), and Phase C (a Pareto-optimal route explorer, the actual EMOA*-flavored research claim) are built on an honest map of the landscape rather than assumption. Concretely: Phase 0/A borrows satvis's and Stuff-in-Space's rendering and worker-propagation idioms directly; Phase B needs to know where Cesium stops (Earth-locked) and what a heliocentric Three.js scene must do differently from spacekit; Phase C needs the landscape scan's central finding — that no actively-maintained open-source project couples an interactive 3D scene to real multi-objective trajectory optimization — to be true, or at least defensible, before it is used as the novelty claim in front of Prof. Rathinam.

Nine reading items were assigned (T3.1–T3.9): satvis (the polish/architecture bar), KeepTrack.space (the raw-WebGL performance ceiling and the AGPL trap), Stuff in Space and five smaller minimal viewers (the "stylized instrument" mood reference), Gpredict/PREDICT (the pass-prediction lineage satvis webified), spacekit.js (the Phase-B heliocentric-base candidate, evaluated and rejected), NASA Eyes on the Solar System (the closed-source "wonder" bar), GMAT (the "real mission design" fidelity ceiling and UI anti-example), CesiumJS idioms/licensing (engine-level facts underlying the Phase A build), and a 417-repository GitHub landscape scan plus the `awesome-space` list (the comparison table and the gap-confirmation scan). A verification pass (two independent lenses: recompute-from-primary-sources, and adversarial source re-checking) was run against six load-bearing claims from this material; corrections from that ledger are folded into the text below and flagged explicitly in §5.

## 3. Core notes, by reading item

### T3.1 — satvis: the polish and architecture bar

satvis (`Flowm/satvis`, branch `next`) is a Vue 3 + Vite 8 + CesiumJS 1.144 (`@cesium/engine` 26.2, `@cesium/widgets` 16.1) + satellite.js 7.1 single-page app, MIT-licensed, live at satvis.space since 2018. As of 2026-09-01: **403 stars, 123 forks, 42 GitHub-reported "open issues"** (verified during the ledger pass to decompose as 39 open issues + 3 open PRs, since GitHub's API field bundles both). Activity is currently extreme: **179 commits in August 2026** — re-verified independently and confirmed as the single highest-volume month in the repo's 8-year history (previous peak 131, July 2026). The historical baseline description "2-15/month" in the original notes is imprecise: excluding the 2018 launch burst and the 2026 ramp, active months on `next` ranged 1-28 commits (median 3), and 41 of 88 months (47%) had *zero* commits — bursty and often dormant, not a steady 2-15/month. What *is* independently confirmed and stronger than the file-presence argument originally used: **148 of the 179 August 2026 commits (83%) carry an explicit `Assisted-by: ClaudeCode:claude-opus-5` trailer**, present from May 2026 onward (20/29/122/148 across May-Aug) — this is direct primary evidence of agent-assisted development, not just circumstantial (the presence of `AGENTS.md`, `CONTEXT.md`, `.claude/launch.json`).

**Architecture** (class names verified in source): `app.ts` → `createViewer()` (Cesium `Viewer`, no animation/timeline widgets, `requestRenderMode`, HDR+ACES) → `CesiumController` → `SatelliteManager` (`reconcile(DesiredScene)`, a diff of store state against the live globe) → `SatelliteCatalog` (dedup key `satnum|name`, tags, lazy group loads) + a `Map<key, SatelliteComponentCollection>` per active satellite (each holding `SatelliteProperties { Orbit(satrec), SampledTrajectory, PassPredictor }` and its component graphics). Pinia stores (`sat.ts`, `cesium.ts`) each declare a `urlsync` schema; `urlSync.ts` ↔ `urlCodec.ts` (a pure codec) round-trips the URL; `sceneSync.ts` is the single file of store→globe watchers. Direction is one-way: store decides, Cesium follows.

**Correction to the master doc** (carried over from the original reader): `worker/` is a **Cloudflare Worker** backend (a 6 h cron pulling CelesTrak into Workers KV, exposing `/api/gp/<group>.json`), not propagation offloading. The off-thread propagation is `src/modules/util/sgp4Worker.ts` (a pool of `min(4, cores-2)` web workers) plus a separate `passWorker.ts`. Also: satvis's URL keys are `sats=`, `tags=`, `gs=`, `track=` — there is no `sat=` singular.

**Position pipeline** (this is the part worth copying line for line). Samples sit on an epoch-anchored grid: t_i = epoch + i·T/120, 120 samples per orbit, window [-0.5, +1.5] orbits = 241 samples, so chunks fetched at different times still interleave exactly. For the ISS, T ≈ 92.98 min gives a 46.5 s step, ≈356 km of travel per sample (script: `notes/t3_1_satvis_numbers.py`). The worker runs `satellite.js.propagate` and then rotates TEME→pseudo-fixed by a Z-rotation using Cesium's own GMST polynomial (`temeToFixed.ts`), so results are bit-identical to `Transforms.computeTemeToPseudoFixedMatrix` (treats UTC as UT1, ignores polar motion). Positions ship as a transferable `Float64Array` in metres. A custom `GridPositionProperty` (uniform grid, 6-point/quintic Lagrange interpolation, `HOLD` at the edges, `STENCIL = 6`) replaces Cesium's stock `SampledPositionProperty`.

**Corrected performance numbers** (see §5 ledger — the original notes rounded this imprecisely). The `GridPositionProperty.ts` header documents a single A/B/A trial at 5,000 satellites, explicitly called out by its own authors as noisy ("a whole benchmark sweep could not resolve this: its own drift check fired at ±23-49% on `cpuMs`"): `dataSourceDisplay.update` = 6.10 ms (this) → 12.02 ms (stock Cesium) → 5.03 ms (this again); fps = 99.9 → 71.1 → 96.8. The number the source itself calls reproducible across separate matched sweeps is a different metric, `tickMs` (propagation + position update): **11.08 ms → 4.91 ms** at 5,000 satellites (11.08/11.44/9.31 before, 4.91/4.63 after). Prefer quoting the `tickMs` figure if pressed on rigor; the `dataSourceDisplay.update`/fps figures are directionally right (a roughly 2× improvement) but come from one noisy trial, not a validated sweep. Accuracy of `GridPositionProperty` vs python-sgp4: 4.7 m error on Starlink, 37.9 m on a 3.5-day-period HEO (MMS-1) — a cubic stencil was 3.7 km off on that same orbit, which is why degree-5 (quintic) was chosen; the error scales roughly as h^(n+1) where h is a fraction of the orbital period.

**Entity vs Primitive.** Point/Label/Model/Cone/Corridor stay Cesium `Entity` graphics; Orbit and Orbit-track lines for every *untracked* satellite are merged `GeometryInstance`s into one batched `Primitive` per line-type (`PolylineBatch.ts`, re-oriented by a single `modelMatrix` update every 0.5 s in the inertial frame). Only the one tracked satellite gets live per-frame `PathGraphics`. Measured: 342 ms/frame for per-entity orbit paths vs 1.24 ms batched, at 5,000 satellites. Rebuilding all orbit-track/ground-track geometry every frame at 5,000 satellites used to cost 342 ms and 414 ms of main thread respectively — hence a throttled refresh interval geometryRefreshSeconds(n) = clamp(n/100, 1 s, 10 s); tightening 10 s → 3 s at 5,000 satellites took the worst frame from 43 ms to 250 ms. Ground track = `CorridorGraphics` (width = swath), sensor cone = `cesium-sensor-volumes` `ConicSensorGraphics` pointed nadir; both LEO-only.

**Orbit-facts panel** (the "professor's lecture as UI" seed). Regime from raw mean motion: e>0.25 ⇒ HEO; T≤128 min ⇒ LEO (circular altitude ≤ 2,035 km); 1400–1470 min ⇒ GEO (35,077–36,448 km); else MEO. Sun-synchronous test via J2 nodal regression, dΩ/dt = -1.5·J2·n·(Re/a)^2·cos(i) / (1-e^2)^2, compared to 0.9856°/day within 0.05°/day — verified: ISS gives -4.947°/day, Sentinel-2 (786 km, i=98.62°) gives +0.994°/day, correctly flagged SSO. LTAN = UT + (Ω − GMST)/15 °/h, shown only when SSO.

**URL state (ADR-0001).** `elements`, `tags`, `sats`, `xsats`, `gs` (`lat,lon[,name]` joined by `_`, 4 dp), `track`, `overpass`, `layers`, `terrain`, `surface`, `stars`, `scene`, `camera`, `pixelratio`, `msaa`, `fps`, `bench`, `bg`, `time`. Rules worth copying: preset supplies defaults, only deviations are emitted; a malformed scalar drops the whole param; an unknown member of a closed list is dropped; user changes `pushState`, clock ticks `replaceState` (throttled); the codec validates on *serialize* too.

**Pitfalls the author already paid for**, worth reusing as our own test/pattern list: don't send the TLE record on every worker request (cache satrecs by satnum, pin one satellite to one worker via FNV-1a lane); don't send one giant `postMessage` of thousands of records (a 133 ms frame at 5,000 — batch into 64-command messages); carry a chunk *anchor + index*, never per-chunk float starts (or two chunks disagree by <1 ms and Cesium sees duplicate samples — observed a 112 s seam); an empty scene is GPU-bound (4× MSAA + HDR at Retina resolution ≈ 13.5 ms of a 14.3 ms frame; default MSAA off at DPR ≥ 2); `PolylineGeometry` with fewer than 2 positions throws *inside the render loop* and kills the app — guard it; the Cesium ion token is needed only for World Terrain / OSM Buildings / Google Photorealistic tiles, and satvis's committed token is domain-restricted to `satvis.space`, so a fresh clone renders fine with the default keyless NaturalEarth base map + ellipsoid terrain.

### T3.2 — KeepTrack.space: the raw-WebGL performance ceiling, and the AGPL trap

KeepTrack (`thkruz/keeptrack.space`, live at app.keeptrack.space) is a mature (created 2016-12-21), very actively maintained (weekly point releases; v13.10.0 pushed 2026-08-08) TypeScript SSA (space situational awareness) tool, primarily by Theodore Kruczek (Kruczek Labs). 1,570 stars, 210 forks, 15 open issues, repo size ~1.16 GB, 92 plugin folders under `src/plugins` plus separate `plugins-pro`/`plugins-external` trees for a commercial tier.

**The single biggest architectural decision**, stated explicitly in `docs/technical.md`: "We have made conscious decisions to avoid large libraries like three.js to fine-tune performance… KeepTrack is capable of modeling more than 2.5 million satellites in real-time using SGP4." This is a from-scratch WebGL2 renderer, not a scene-graph engine. The satellite cloud is drawn as raw `gl.POINTS` (confirmed: three separate `gl.drawArrays(gl.POINTS, 0, drawCount)` call sites in `dots-manager.ts` — main draw, GPU-picking pass, hover/reticle pass). Each point's screen size and color come from per-vertex attributes computed in the vertex shader (`DotStyle` enum — SoftGlow/Disc/Ring/Diamond/Square — with per-status size codes baked into an `Int8Array`, so highlighting a searched/selected/hovered satellite costs zero extra draw calls). Object picking uses a second off-screen colour-ID pass, read back over a **15×15 pixel box** (widened from 1×1 — a single-pixel picking buffer went stale between the draw frame and the async GPU readback, making isolated/sparse objects unclickable; the fix trades ~225 fragments for click reliability). Only a handful of hero objects (Earth, Moon, Sun, planets, dwarf planets with Chebyshev-polynomial ephemerides, sensor FOV cones, selected-satellite model) are real mesh geometry.

**Propagation architecture.** `src/webworker/` holds one dedicated Web Worker per computation: `positionCruncher.ts` (SGP4 for the whole catalog every tick), `orbitCruncher.ts` (orbit-path generation), plus specialized workers (`azRangeHeatmapWorker`, `bestPassWorker`, `closeObjectsWorker`, `debrisScreeningWorker`, `proximityOpsWorker`, `time2lonWorker`) and an optional WASM SGP4 backend. Propagation itself is Kruczek's own `@ootk` TypeScript library, descended from `satellite.js`/`suncalc` — i.e. the *same algorithm family* satvis uses, wrapped differently. Position and velocity ship main-thread-bound as Transferable `Float32Array` (`postMessage(msg, { transfer: [...] })`), **deliberately not `SharedArrayBuffer`**: a code comment states "SharedArrayBuffer stays off the table: COOP/COEP would break embeds." This is a directly reusable lesson for our own worker design.

**Data fusion.** Explicit source-priority order in `catalog-loader.ts`: Space-Track/USSF > CelesTrak > CelesTrak-Supplemental > Vimpel > SatNOGS, higher-priority sources overwrite lower ones per-NORAD-ID.

**License, confirmed and re-verified during the ledger pass**: AGPL-3.0-or-later — this is the exact SPDX-qualified license, confirmed by reading both the raw repo LICENSE file and (since the API's `spdx_id` field only returns the ambiguous "AGPL-3.0" shorthand) the README's own prose statement: "KeepTrack is licensed under the **GNU Affero General Public License v3.0 or later**." Per-file confirmation on an actual worker file (`positionCruncher.ts` header) resolves to AGPL-3.0-or-later at the source-file level too. AGPL's network-use clause means running a modified version as a network service (which a web app always is) requires releasing the combined source. **Practical rule for this project: do not copy KeepTrack source — shaders, worker code, any file — into our MIT-licensed repo**; study the architecture and reimplement independently. One nuance the verification pass surfaced: KeepTrack's maintainer explicitly offers a commercial-licensing escape hatch ("Need a different license? Contact Kruczek Labs") — so the AGPL default is not the only path, but absent a negotiated agreement it is the operative constraint.

**Performance-ceiling framing.** Cesium's Entity API is known (and confirmed by the T3.8 CesiumJS item, see below) to bog down in the low thousands of dynamic entities because of per-object CPU-side property re-evaluation and scene-graph bookkeeping. KeepTrack's raw `gl.POINTS` + one-shot VBO update bypasses all of that: one JS-side array write per satellite per frame, one GPU draw call for the whole catalog. This structural difference — not cleverer shaders — is why KeepTrack can credibly claim 2.5M objects at 60 fps (self-reported vendor claim, not independently reproduced by any reader; treat as a marketing ceiling) with the everyday, credible figure being "50,000+ satellites tracked in real-time" (also self-reported).

### T3.3 — Stuff in Space and the minimal-viewer genre: the "stylized instrument" mood reference

**Stuff in Space (jeyoder)**, the original, is ~5,300 lines of hand-written WebGL1 + jQuery, no framework, no bundler. It has **no LICENSE file** (GitHub API `license: null`; treat as all-rights-reserved — learn from it, do not copy it). Its fork **ajmas/StuffInSpace (TS + Three.js + Vite) is MIT**. Activity: 51 commits total, last commit 2019-12-15; 834 stars, 150 forks. Critically, **stuffin.space is effectively dead**: a curl probe on 2026-09-01 got HTTP 301 with an empty Location and 0-byte assets; `/TLE.json` and `/scripts/main.js` return 404 (`notes/T3_3_stuffinspace_live_output.txt`). The maintained successor `ajmas.github.io/StuffInSpace` is live, but its bundled `data/TLE.json` (27.7 MB) has an epoch ~255 days stale as of this reading.

**Architecture (two web workers, one draw loop).** `sat-cruncher.js` runs SGP4 on the whole catalog every **500 ms** and posts transferable `Float32Array` buffers; the main thread linearly extrapolates `pos += vel·dt` between updates (script check: at 60 fps this error is roughly (1/2)·a_c·dt^2, a_c = mu/r^2 — 1.08 m at 0.5 s for the ISS, invisible at the ~7.7 km/px scale a typical camera distance implies). `orbit-calculation-worker.js` samples one full period at **255 equal-time slices** for the selected orbit's `LINE_STRIP`. This equal-time sampling has a documented flaw (the author's own TODO): "ugly perigees on HEOs" — computed independently, a GTO (e=0.73) gets a 13.1° true-anomaly step at perigee vs 0.32° at apogee (41×), producing a visible ~43 km / 5.7 px kink; the fix is to sample by true or eccentric anomaly, not time.

**The look, quantified.** Point sprite size: `gl_PointSize = clamp(320000/w, 7.5, 20)` px (20 px until ~16,000 km depth, 7.5 px floor beyond ~42,700 km — dots never vanish). Alpha falloff: `min((1 − |pointCoord| + 0.1)^3, 1)` — a soft cubic (0.61 at 25% radius, 0.22 at 50%, 0.04 at 75%). Blending is **normal** alpha (`SRC_ALPHA, ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA`, `depthMask(false)`), not additive. Class colours: payload (1,.2,0,1), rocket body (.2,.5,1,.85), debris (.5,.5,.5,.85); group mode dims non-members to alpha 0.1 and makes them unpickable — "dim, don't hide" is the single biggest readability win in this codebase. Zoom curve: camera distance = zoom^3·(200000-6400)+6400 km — a cubic ease so most slider travel is spent near Earth. Compare KeepTrack's dot shader: `clamp(2·(0.53 − |p|) + 0.5, 0, 1)` — a wider, harder-edged core (half-alpha at 53% radius vs 31% here) and size ∝ (14000/depth)^2.1 clamped 5.5–48 px.

**Design rules for the "stylized instrument" aesthetic** distilled from these sources: draw the catalog as one `gl.POINTS`/`Points` shader-disc draw with a pixel-clamped size floor; use normal-alpha for the colour-coded core and additive only for a dim (alpha ≤ 0.2) glow halo; sample orbit lines by true anomaly (≥128 segments), only build them for hovered/selected/grouped objects; dim non-matches rather than hiding them; cap labels (~40 on 1080p, 24 px separation) and render as DOM overlays; propagate at ~2 Hz in a worker and extrapolate per frame (error is sub-pixel); cubic-ease the zoom slider so most travel stays near Earth; carry URL state from day one. Pitfalls to encode as tests: mixed Earth radii (6371 vs 6378.137 km), WGS-72 vs WGS-84 mu in SGP4-derived elements, ECI vs ECEF orbit lines, and a staleness banner (every Stuff-in-Space-lineage site ships a stale TLE).

### T3.4 — Gpredict and PREDICT: the pass-prediction lineage

**PREDICT** (John Magliacane, KD2BD): work began 1991, first public DOS release 1994, v2.0.0 rewrite 1999; latest release 2.2.5 (2018-05-07), effectively frozen since. C, ncurses, ~6.7k-line `predict.c`; docs say GPL-2-or-later, repo LICENSE file is GPLv3 text. Its SGP4/SDP4 is the Kelso/Kyriazis C port; Gpredict bundles the same lineage code (`src/sgpsdp/README`: "based on sgp4sdp4-0.3 by Neoklis Kyriazis"), and `satellite.js` is the JavaScript port of the same family — so **PREDICT → Gpredict → satellite.js → satvis is one straight propagator lineage**.

**Gpredict** (Alexandru Csete, OZ9AEC): GTK3/Cairo desktop app, C, GPL-2.0. Very much alive: 2.4 (Dec 2025), 2.5/2.5.1 (Mar 2026), 2.5.2 (Jul 2026), 2.6 (Aug 2026); 1.2k GitHub stars, 295 forks.

**The operator workflow satvis webified.** (1) *Get elements*: PREDICT hard-caps at 24 satellites; Gpredict defaults to CelesTrak's amateur/cubesat/visual/weather files, weekly auto-update-with-notify. (2) *Define the ground station*: PREDICT uses degrees-**west**-positive longitude (a real pitfall); Gpredict is east-positive and supports GPSD. (3) *Predict passes*: Gpredict defaults — `MINIMUM_ELEV = 5°`, `NUMBER_OF_PASSES = 10`, `LOOK_AHEAD = 3` days, `TIME_RESOLUTION = 10` s, `TWILIGHT_THRESHOLD = -6°`. Multi-pass table columns: AOS, TCA, LOS, Duration, Max El, AOS/Max-El/LOS Az, Orbit, Vis (V/D/E). (4) *Watch live*: List, Map (footprint, ground track, terminator since 2.4, next-event countdown), Polar/radar, Single-Sat, "Sky at a Glance" (a timeline strip of upcoming passes — the direct ancestor of satvis's `PassTimeline.vue`). (5) *Drive hardware*: Hamlib `rigctld`/`rotctld` TCP; Doppler formula df_down = -f_down·rhodot/c, df_up = +f_up·rhodot/c. PREDICT even exposes a UDP server on **port 1210** with ASCII commands (`GET_SAT`, `GET_DOPPLER`, etc.) — the 1999 answer to "one propagator, many clients."

**The math a v1 needs** (all reproduced in `notes/t3_4_pass_math.py`). Look angles (Vallado Alg. 27; identical to satellite.js `ecfToLookAngles` and Gpredict's `Calculate_Obs`): rotate rho = r_sat,ECEF - r_obs into the topocentric SEZ frame, then el = arcsin(Z/rho), az = atan2(E,-S) mod 360°. Footprint radius from altitude: lambda = arccos((R_E/(R_E+h))·cos(eps)) - eps; ground radius = R_E·lambda. Verified: ISS (420 km, eps=0) → lambda = 20.25°, radius 2,254 km, matching PREDICT's own `fk = 12756.33·acos(xkmper/(xkmper+alt))` formula exactly (4507.46 km diameter). With a 5° mask the ISS footprint shrinks to 1,762 km. A brute-force pass search (10 s steps, 3 days, ISS over College Station 30.6187°N/-96.3365°E, 5° mask) found 14 passes, first AOS 04:19:20 Z, max el 72.1°, LOS 04:27:50 Z — matched against `skyfield.find_events` with the same mask (04:19:13/72.4°/04:27:41), agreeing within the 10 s sampling step. Cost: 5,760 SGP4 evaluations for a 4-day/60-s sweep took 6.5 ms in Python; satvis quotes ~8 ms in JS per satellite per station — cheap for one satellite, but ×thousands it drives the need for a dedicated worker (satvis: ~40 s for 5,000 satellites against one station).

**satvis's version.** `Orbit.computePassesElevation()` — 5° min elevation, 50-pass cap, adaptive stepping (5 min steps far from horizon down to 2 s inside the pass) via `satellite.js ecfToLookAngles`, run in a dedicated `passWorker.ts`. A second "swath" mode predicts sensor-footprint overpasses with per-side cross-track extents — beyond what either desktop tool ever did. Ground-station link polyline is shown only inside `passIntervals` (a Cesium `TimeIntervalCollection`). Notifications: Web Notifications API via service worker, `setTimeout` at T-5 min and T-0, deduplicated within 10 s; on iOS a WKWebView wrapper bridges to `UNUserNotificationCenter`.

### T3.5 — spacekit.js: evaluated and rejected as a Phase B base

spacekit (`typpo/spacekit`, ~6,157 lines TS, MIT, Ian Webster) wraps **three.js r0.135.0 (Dec 2021)** with `WebGL1Renderer`. `Ephem` stores everything in radians internally (accepts deg/rad), distances in AU, time in Julian Date; it auto-fills missing quantities exactly like a standard Curtis-style element normalization (q=a(1-e), wbar=Om+w, L=Om+w+M, n=2pi/period). Kepler's equation is solved by fixed-point iteration E_(k+1) = M + e·sin(E_k) (tol 1e-7, cap 100 — linear convergence rate ~e; independently reproduced: 4 iterations at e=0.0167 but 16 at e=0.99, vs 3-6 for Newton-Raphson) duplicated in GLSL for a `KeplerParticles` cloud (one `THREE.Points`, per-particle element `BufferAttributes`, the GPU solves Kepler per particle each frame — a genuinely good pattern for 10^4–10^5 bodies, this is how Asterank draws the whole MPC catalog).

**Ephemerides are the real problem.** `EphemPresets` hard-codes one **fixed osculating element set per planet at JD 2458426.5 (2018-11-04)** from Horizons (Earth from JPL's `p_elem_t1.txt` J2000 mean elements, *without* secular rate terms) — not SPK/DE kernels, not VSOP87. Independently checked against Skyfield DE421 on 2026-09-01 (`notes/spacekit_accuracy_check.py`): Mercury/Venus/Mars reproduce the library's own math to 0-3 km at the preset epoch (confirming the reimplementation), but propagated two-body to today the error is **144,000 km for Earth, 992,000 km for Jupiter, and 12.4 million km (0.38°) for Saturn**. Invisible at solar-system zoom, but unusable as direct input to a Lambert/porkchop solver, where a 0.1 AU planet-position error corrupts C3/v_infinity at a km²/s² level. The honest alternative in the library, `EphemerisTable` ([JD, x, y, z, vx, vy, vz], Lagrange interpolation order 1-20, default 5), has no built-in SPK loader — you have to generate the JSON yourself from Horizons/SPICE.

**Scale.** Scene unit defaults to 1 AU with a single **global linear** scale factor; no log-scale/compressed-distance mode exists anywhere (grep of the whole source for "log"/"logarithm" finds nothing but `cbrt`). float32 ULP at 1 AU is **17.8 km** — 4.5% of a 400 km LEO altitude — so a heliocentric float32 scene cannot host a LEO orbit without a floating-origin redesign, which spacekit does not have. Camera near/far = 1e-5 AU (~1,500 km) to 2000 AU, a 2e8 ratio, with `logarithmicDepthBuffer` commented out in the source (z-fighting on planet close-ups).

**Maintenance status.** 581 stars, 47 forks, 18 open issues; npm has only two releases (0.1.0 in 2021, 0.1.1 in 2023); commit history is quiet from Nov 2023 to Apr 2026, then a one-week correctness-fix burst by the maintainer, then quiet again (last push 2026-04-10). three.js r163 removed WebGL1 support entirely; the one community upgrade PR (#77, three 0.173/WebGL2) has sat unanswered for over a year. Open bug #76: objects with e>0.9 render misplaced.

**Verdict: borrow patterns, do not build on it.** Reasons: dead WebGL1 stack; planetary positions already wrong by 10^5–10^7 km; no log-scale mode; float32-AU incompatible with any Earth-orbit satellite content; small enough (Ephem 358 lines, Orbit 676, KeplerParticles 355) that the useful ideas port to a fresh Three.js scene in an afternoon under MIT.

### T3.6 — NASA Eyes on the Solar System: the wonder bar, not the architecture bar

Eyes on the Solar System (`eyes.nasa.gov/apps/solar-system/`) renders 126 NASA spacecraft (past and present) across a 1949–2049 date range; the 2023 relaunch took "more than two years" to develop and is fully browser-based with no plugin. Feature set worth cataloguing: a **playback-rate dial** (not just play/pause, from the original 2010 launch); a huge, populated spacecraft catalog; a **ride-along camera** attaching to a moving spacecraft; scripted **guided narrative tours** ("Voyager's Grand Tour") layered over real trajectory data; object rotate/compare/relight; and cross-domain consistency — the same shell (timeline + camera + object picker) powers sibling apps **Eyes on Asteroids** (~43,000 CNEOS NEOs, updated daily) and **Eyes on the Earth** (ride-along satellite view over a data-draped globe, 8 "vital signs," up to 20 years of playback).

**Honest gaps in this item's own research**, worth stating plainly rather than papering over: the exact rendering engine behind the current (post-Blend4Web) build is not disclosed anywhere in JPL's public materials — multiple targeted searches turned up nothing. History confirms at least three engine changes: Unity (2010, required install — explicitly why later versions moved to browser), Java (mid-2012), Blend4Web (a Blender-integrated WebGL framework, added later). Whether Eyes ingests SPICE kernels directly is architecturally likely (JPL originated SPICE; NAIF's own official SPICE visualizer, Cosmographia, is a *separate* desktop tool) but not confirmed by any Eyes-specific document.

**Framing for the meeting.** Eyes is the correct "wonder" reference precisely because it is unreachable as an architecture reference: closed-source, built by a dedicated JPL team over years, with proprietary access to every mission's SPICE kernels. The professor's own framing ("satvis is the bar for polish/architecture; NASA Eyes is the bar for wonder") is the right takeaway. Concrete, copyable patterns, ranked by reach: (1) a visible time-rate dial on top of Cesium's clock — cheap, satvis already half-demonstrates this; (2) trajectory arcs with discrete encounter/closest-approach markers, feeding naturally off a future Lambert/porkchop layer's output; (3) an explicit "not to scale" distance toggle rather than trying to out-render Eyes; (4) one scripted guided tour (e.g. a Hohmann or EMOA*-chosen route replayed with camera + narration) as the single highest-leverage demo feature. Explicitly out of reach and correctly deprioritized: matching the 126-spacecraft historical catalog breadth (a multi-decade JPL data-production effort), a literal "real-time" wall-clock sync (irrelevant for hypothetical optimizer trajectories), and any attempt to match Eyes' rendering fidelity with a custom engine.

### T3.7 — GMAT: what real mission design includes, and the UI anti-example

GMAT (NASA Goddard's General Mission Analysis Tool) is **Apache-2.0** (independently re-verified during the ledger pass by reading `License.txt` directly, not just the GitHub badge — confirmed genuine Apache License 2.0 text). It has flown real support for 8+ NASA missions and is the closest thing to a "real mission design tool" in open source — and simultaneously the UI anti-example the project should not imitate.

**Architecture.** GMAT is built around **Resources** (Spacecraft, Propagator, ImpulsiveBurn, FiniteBurn, Thruster, Tank, GroundStation, DifferentialCorrector, Optimizer, CoordinateSystem, …) and a scripted **Mission Sequence** (Propagate, Target, Optimize, Maneuver, Vary, Achieve, Minimize, NonlinearConstraint, …). Every GUI-built model has an exact textual script equivalent, always in sync — a "missions are procedures, not static state" model, unlike a satvis-style viewer. The GUI itself is wxWidgets — a cross-platform native-widget toolkit organized around a Resources tree / Mission tree / Command Summary / Script Editor, the classic 2000s desktop-engineering-tool layout. (Note: no forum/review source calling this UI "clunky" in so many words was independently found — that specific framing is inference from the verifiable architecture, not a quoted review.)

**Fidelity GMAT has that any v1 will honestly omit** (this is the deliverable to lead with): analytic propagators (two-body Kepler, SGP4/SDP4) *and* numerical high-precision integrators (RK8(9), Prince-Dormand, Bulirsch-Stoer) with configurable force models — spherical-harmonic gravity to arbitrary degree/order, atmospheric drag (Jacchia-Roberts, MSISE90), solar radiation pressure with eclipse shadow modeling, relativistic corrections; **FiniteBurn** (real thrust duration, mass depletion, thrust-vector profile) alongside `ImpulsiveBurn`; **B-plane targeting** (the flagship Mars tutorial: matching B·T/B·R hyperbolic-approach conditions via a differential-correction Targeter, distinct from GMAT's Optimizer, which is an NLP solver — VF13ad built-in, optional fmincon/SNOPT/IPOPT plugins); navigation/orbit-determination with covariance propagation; ground-station contact scheduling with link budgets; multi-spacecraft formation flying; real hardware modeling (tanks, thrusters, mass properties).

**Suggested disclosure language** for the project's own README/about page: *"This tool uses simplified two-body/patched-conic trajectory models for visualization and multi-objective route comparison. It does not model finite burns, atmospheric drag, non-spherical gravity, solar radiation pressure, B-plane targeting, navigation uncertainty, or mission-level Δv budgets — the kind of fidelity found in tools like NASA's GMAT. Treat outputs as first-order mission-design exploration, not flight-ready trajectories."*

**Distribution footprint, re-verified with corrections during the ledger pass.** GMAT's `LICENSE` is confirmed Apache 2.0 by direct read. On sizes: independently re-fetched the SourceForge R2025a release via its RSS/XML feed (exact byte counts, not just the rendered HTML page) — Windows 428.67 MB, macOS 402.75 MB, RHEL7 461.21 MB, Ubuntu 421.74 MB, all matching the original figures to within 0.05 MB. **Summing all 8 listed files gives 2.389 GB (decimal/SI convention) — "approximately 2.4 GB" checks out arithmetically** — but this figure double-counts: `GMAT-src_and_data-R2025a.zip` (339.1 MB) is almost certainly a repackaging of `GMAT-src-R2025a.tar.gz` (136.4 MB) + `GMAT-datafiles-R2025a.tar.gz` (199.2 MB), which sum to 335.6 MB, nearly identical. Excluding that redundant bundle gives ~2.05 GB. More importantly, **no single user downloads all 8 files** — installing GMAT on one platform means downloading exactly one ~400–460 MB installer, not 2.4 GB. Correct framing: *"GMAT R2025a is Apache-2.0 licensed; a single-platform install is ~400-460 MB, and the SourceForge release page as a whole totals ~2.2-2.4 GB across all platforms/source/data (with some redundancy in the src+data bundle)."*

**Refined gap statement** (important correction, carried from T3.7's own self-check): the claim "no open-source tool couples optimization to visualization" is too broad and GMAT is itself a counterexample to the broad form — GMAT *does* couple optimization (Targeters/DC, NLP Optimizers) to 3D visualization (OrbitView) in one application. The correct, narrower, and defensible claim for the Monday meeting is: **GMAT couples continuous single-trajectory shooting/NLP optimization to a legacy OpenGL 3D view; no open-source tool couples *discrete multi-objective Pareto-front graph search* (EMOA*-style) to interactive 3D/porkchop visualization.** This narrower framing survives scrutiny; the broad one does not.

### T3.8 — CesiumJS idioms, licensing, and token-free setup

CesiumJS (`cesium` npm, satvis pinned at 1.144.0; modern consumption is `@cesium/engine` 26.2 + `@cesium/widgets` 16.1, the scoped-package split rather than the monolithic meta-package) is **Apache 2.0** — free for commercial and non-commercial use, no attribution requirement. Cesium ion (the hosted asset/terrain/imagery SaaS) is a *separate* metered product with its own ToS; the rendering engine itself is fully usable with zero ion dependency. Minified bundle size: **4.2 MB** (Scene 49%, Core 19%, DataSources 9% — quoted directly from satvis's own `vite.config.js` comment).

**Token-free default setup, exactly as satvis ships it.** Base imagery: a `TileMapServiceImageryProvider` over a self-vendored, pre-tiled copy of Cesium's own bundled Natural Earth II tiles (levels 0-2 committed as WebP, 3-5 generated by a build script) — deliberately vendored rather than using the packaged copy inside `@cesium/engine` directly, because above the generated max level Cesium's `TileImagery` walks up to the nearest ready ancestor and magnifies it rather than showing a hole ("what makes a build without the generated levels soft instead of broken"). Other keyless imagery wired in: `ArcGisMapServerImageryProvider` (Esri World_Imagery), a `UrlTemplateImageryProvider` against VersaTiles (satellite XYZ, 512 px tiles — discovered by fetching an actual tile and inspecting it, not assumed), `OpenStreetMapImageryProvider`, and a `WebMapServiceImageryProvider` against NASA GIBS for VIIRS Black Marble night-lights. Terrain: `EllipsoidTerrainProvider` (flat, token-free — this is Cesium's actual zero-relief default) or `ArcGISTiledElevationTerrainProvider`; only `createWorldTerrainAsync` (Cesium World Terrain) needs the ion token, and it needs vertex normals because `globe.enableLighting` is on — without them, terrain shades flat off the ellipsoid. **Recommended minimal setup for this project:** `@cesium/engine` + `@cesium/widgets`; bundled `TileMapServiceImageryProvider` over `NaturalEarthII` as the offline-safe default, `OpenStreetMapImageryProvider` as an optional toggle; `EllipsoidTerrainProvider`; never set `Ion.defaultAccessToken`, and wrap any future ion-dependent feature behind a try/catch that degrades to a UI toast (satvis's pattern) rather than throwing into the render loop.

**Idioms to copy.** `SampledPositionProperty` + `LagrangePolynomialApproximation` (imported from `@cesium/engine` rather than the default linear interpolation) for smooth curved trajectories — with a documented pitfall: "Cesium 1.143 widened the `InterpolationAlgorithm` interface without updating the `LagrangePolynomialApproximation` namespace declaration," forcing a manual type cast — expect type/runtime drift across Cesium point releases and pin exact versions. `CallbackProperty` for live sensor-cone orientation, with an explicit guard for `position === undefined` (feeding `undefined` into `Transforms.headingPitchRollQuaternion` throws a `DeveloperError` **inside the render loop**, silently killing rendering for the rest of the session — a nasty pitfall to test for explicitly). The Entity-vs-Primitive threshold is stated in satvis's own code: `orbitUsesPathGraphic(isTracked, sceneModeSupportsPrimitive)` — "required for the tracked satellite and for scene modes without primitive model-matrix updates; all other satellites use the significantly faster primitive."

**External community numbers on the Entity-API ceiling** (lower confidence than satvis's own measurements, since these are third-party reports): Cesium's own 2018 "Entity API Performance" blog post benchmarks 15,000 billboard+label entities, showing a "dirty flag" optimization moving main-thread idle time from ~50% to ~95% — it explicitly declines to give a universal safe-entity-count threshold. A Cesium community forum thread reports **5 fps with as few as 1 entity** using `SampledPositionProperty` + `PathGraphics`, root-caused to path-resolution/granularity settings, not entity count ("with a small set of points, and a low resolution set, there was a lot of interpolation calculation") — fixable with `ArcType.NONE` and coarser resolution. A second forum thread (not independently opened, lower confidence) reports ~150,000 entity-path points dropping to 2 fps.

**KeepTrack comparison for the landscape.** KeepTrack is not Cesium-based (custom WebGL2, AGPL-3.0-or-later) — its README claims are the ceiling reference for "what a purpose-built renderer buys you," roughly 2-3 orders of magnitude beyond what Cesium's Entity API handles smoothly, at the cost of owning 100% of the rendering stack and inheriting a copyleft license. For this project's Phase A target (hundreds to low-thousands of tracked, richly interactive objects), Cesium's Entity API is the right call; KeepTrack's architecture only pays for itself past tens-of-thousands of objects with minimal per-object interactivity, which is not the Phase A/B target.

**poliastro is archived** (2023-10-14; independently re-confirmed during the ledger pass by reading the repo's own README, which states "poliastro is archived and will not be developed any further," plus the maintainer's archival issue #1640: "poliastro effectively has zero maintainers at the moment, and it's unlikely either of us will return… or ever"). Its community-initiated fork `hapsira` (not officially endorsed by poliastro's maintainers — issue #1640 names no successor) has 42 stars and its default `main` branch's last commit is 2024-01-15, roughly 2 years 4 months stale as of this writing — genuinely worse than the headline "last pushed 2024-05-09" suggests, since that push landed on a non-default branch. **Recommendation reaffirmed: prefer `pykep` (esa/pykep, MPL-2.0, actively pushed 2026-07-07, not archived) for any dependency on Lambert/optimization math**, and treat any poliastro-lineage dependency as a real technical risk.

## 4. Answers to the track questions

### Q1. What exact feature set makes satvis feel polished, and which 3 features beyond parity would differentiate this project?

**The polish inventory**, condensed from §3's T3.1 section: 14 CelesTrak catalog groups with lazy loading and a virtualized 12,000+-entry search; 7 per-satellite render components (Point/Label/Orbit/Orbit-track/Ground-track/Sensor-cone/3D-model) with an Entity-for-few, batched-Primitive-for-many split that keeps 5,000+ satellites interactive; an off-thread SGP4 worker pool on an epoch-anchored sample grid with a custom quintic-Lagrange position property (measured ~2× render-cost improvement over stock Cesium at 5,000 satellites, and a more rigorously reproduced ~2.3× improvement on the propagation-tick half of the frame, 11.08 → 4.91 ms — see §5's correction on the exact figures); an orbit-facts panel that derives regime, sun-synchronicity, and LTAN from raw elements rather than just displaying them; a full pass-prediction and notification pipeline (worker-isolated, elevation and swath modes, browser + iOS notifications); ADR-governed, fully shareable URL state; and a keyless default so a fresh clone renders immediately. Activity/health is itself part of the polish: MIT, 403 stars, still being actively pushed the day it was read, with 83% of its recent commit surge directly attributable (via commit trailers, independently confirmed) to agent-assisted development.

**Evaluating the three candidate differentiators against this baseline:**

1. **Element-sandbox teaching mode — genuine gap, cheapest to build.** Satvis has no Keplerian propagator and no read/write element editor: every object originates from a real satrec, and the orbit-facts panel is read-only. Nothing across the whole genre surveyed (satvis, KeepTrack, Stuff in Space, spacekit, Gpredict, PREDICT) lets a user drag a, e, i, Omega, omega, nu and watch an orbit deform live. Since a Phase 0 Kepler core is needed anyway for the project's non-Earth-orbit legs, exposing it as an interactive sandbox is nearly free — the highest differentiation-per-effort of the three, buildable in Phase 0/A on a timescale of weeks.

2. **Linked porkchop-to-3D — beyond parity, needs new architecture.** None of the established open-source viewers surveyed has a plotting panel wired to click-to-select a trajectory in a 3D scene. However, the companion gap analysis (gap_statement.md) found four single-author repositories created in 2026 that do couple a single-objective Lambert porkchop grid to a Three.js or canvas solar-system view (chipi/orrery, kevinkicho/k-solar-system-navigator, abc000cool/porkchop-mission-planner, tamiralmas/Orbital-Simulation; 0 to 3 stars each, three of the four disclosing heavy AI-assisted authorship, one with no license file). So the defensible differentiator is not "any porkchop-to-3D link" but a porkchop-to-3D link that is validated against oracles, maintained, and later fed by multi-objective (Pareto) search. Satvis is architecturally Earth-locked (Cesium's "inertial" mode is only a rotation of the Earth-fixed frame; there is no heliocentric origin), so this differentiator belongs to the Phase B heliocentric Three.js scene, not a Cesium extension. GMAT is the only tool anywhere coupling optimization to a 3D view, and only for one continuous shooting-method trajectory at a time, via a legacy desktop UI. The T3.9 landscape scan corroborates this is a real, mostly-empty niche — though see §5 for a correction softening "entirely absent" to "no strong counterexample found," since one small (14-star) project, `abolfazlshirazi/ZERUA`, claims a similar coupling and was not caught by the original scan.

3. **Pareto route explorer — entirely absent from the genre surveyed, the biggest lift.** Nothing surveyed does discrete multi-objective (EMOA*-class) route search at all — not satvis, not GMAT (single-objective differential-correction/NLP on one trajectory), not pykep/pagmo2 (Lambert/global optimization with static-plot output). Satvis's pass-timeline + entity-info-panel + URL-state pattern (a selected item = a selected entity with tabs, shareable via URL) is the UI substrate this feature should reuse. This is the most novel claim and also the most research-heavy — properly a Phase C deliverable.

**Ranking by differentiation-per-effort:** element sandbox (Phase 0/A, weeks) → porkchop-to-3D link (Phase B, needs the heliocentric scene) → Pareto route explorer (Phase C, the actual research contribution).

### Q2. Cesium vs custom WebGL (KeepTrack) vs Three.js (spacekit): at what object count and which feature does each break down?

**CesiumJS.** Naive Cesium (per-entity `SampledPositionProperty`, per-entity `PathGraphics`, `CallbackProperty`-driven corridors) breaks down in the **low thousands**: satvis's own code comments record 342 ms/frame for per-entity orbit paths and 414 ms/frame for per-entity corridor callbacks at 5,000 satellites, plus a 908 ms single-frame freeze instantiating 5,000 Entities from scratch. This matches the wider community record: Cesium's 2018 Entity-API blog post needed a "dirty flag" optimization just to get 15,000 billboard+label entities to ~95% idle time; a community forum thread found 5 fps with as few as **1** dynamic entity when path-resolution settings were wrong (root cause: interpolation cost, not count). With satvis's optimizations layered on (worker-pool SGP4, custom quintic `GridPositionProperty`, batched Primitives for bulk orbit lines), Cesium comfortably holds **~5,000–12,000** interactive, individually-clickable satellites at 71–97 fps on a laptop-class GPU — the remaining bottleneck at that scale is CPU-side `dataSourceDisplay.update`, not the GPU (an empty scene alone is ~14.3 ms/frame at 4× MSAA + HDR). **Feature-level breakdown, not just count**: Cesium's scene is fundamentally WGS84/Earth-centred; "inertial camera" is only a rotation applied to the Earth-fixed frame, with no heliocentric origin and no log-scale distance mode anywhere in the API — satvis never attempts a heliocentric view, and this is architectural, not a performance ceiling.

**Custom WebGL (KeepTrack).** One `gl.POINTS` draw call for the entire catalog, worker-computed positions, zero per-object scene-graph bookkeeping — structurally O(objects) in memory bandwidth only, not in JS property re-evaluation or draw calls. This is why KeepTrack's own README claims 50,000+ satellites tracked live and up to 2.5 million simulated debris objects at 60 fps (self-reported, not independently benchmarked by any reader in this track — treat as a marketing ceiling; 50,000+ is the credible everyday figure). It breaks down on **license** (AGPL-3.0-or-later, confirmed and re-confirmed during verification — nothing from its source can be copied into an MIT project without forcing the combination to relicense) and on **interactivity richness** (raw colour-ID points, not a rich per-object property/graphics API), not on raw object count.

**Three.js (spacekit specifically — not Three.js in general).** spacekit's `KeplerParticles` pattern (one `THREE.Points`, per-particle element attributes, GPU-side Kepler solve) is architecturally sound to 10^4–10^5 bodies (this is how Asterank renders the whole MPC catalog), so Three.js itself does not break down on count with the right pattern. spacekit specifically breaks down on: **stack currency** (three r0.135/WebGL1, and three r163 removed WebGL1 entirely — a hard platform dead end, independent of scale); **scale precision** (float32 ULP at 1 AU is 17.8 km, 4.5% of a LEO altitude — a heliocentric float32 scene cannot host a LEO orbit without a floating-origin redesign, which spacekit lacks); **depth** (near/far ratio 2e8 with `logarithmicDepthBuffer` disabled — z-fighting on close-ups); and **ephemeris accuracy** (fixed 2018 osculating elements already off by 0.38° for Saturn as of 2026, unusable for Lambert-quality inputs). A modern hand-rolled Three.js scene — not spacekit — remains the right Phase B choice, reusing spacekit's `Ephem`/`EphemerisTable`/Kepler-particle-cloud patterns under MIT but rebuilding scale handling and sourcing JPL-quality ephemeris tables instead of spacekit's fixed elements.

**Net comparison for the meeting:** Cesium (satvis-tuned) breaks down architecturally at "leaving Earth," not at count, comfortably handling ~5,000-12,000 interactive objects; custom WebGL (KeepTrack) breaks down on license and interactivity richness, reaching 50,000+ objects credibly; Three.js (spacekit specifically) breaks down on stack currency and scale precision, not on the underlying GPU pattern.

## 6. Implications for the build

**Phase 0 (element sandbox demo).** Build the Kepler-core + interactive element editor now — this is the single highest differentiation-per-effort feature identified across the entire track, and nothing surveyed (satvis, KeepTrack, spacekit, Gpredict, PREDICT) has it. Reuse spacekit's `Ephem` normalization rules (q=a(1-e), wbar=Om+w, L=Om+w+M, radians internally, AU/JD units) as the element-class design, but use Newton-Raphson (not spacekit's fixed-point iteration) for Kepler's equation — fixed-point converges linearly at rate ~e and needs 16 iterations at e=0.99 vs 3-6 for Newton.

**Phase A (satvis-parity Earth-orbit viewer, Cesium).**
- Copy directly: epoch-anchored sample grid + worker-pool SGP4 + transferable-`Float64Array` pipeline (satvis's `sgp4Worker.ts`/`sampleSource.ts`); a custom quintic-Lagrange `GridPositionProperty` instead of stock `SampledPositionProperty` once object count exceeds a few hundred; the Entity-for-few/batched-Primitive-for-many split for orbit lines; the ADR-0001 URL contract (preset defaults, deviations only, validate on serialize); the keyless default (bundled NaturalEarth + `EllipsoidTerrainProvider`, ion only for optional layers, wrapped in try/catch → toast, never a throw into the render loop).
- Rendering budget rule to encode as a design constraint: naive per-entity Cesium breaks down around 1,000-5,000 dynamic objects (342-414 ms/frame at 5,000 in satvis's own tests); plan the batched-Primitive path from day one rather than retrofitting it once the demo catalog grows.
- Concrete pitfalls to write as guards/tests: `PolylineGeometry` with <2 positions throws inside the render loop — guard it; `CallbackProperty` fed `position === undefined` throws a `DeveloperError` inside the render loop — guard it; carry chunk *anchor + index*, never per-chunk float starts, to avoid duplicate-sample seams; cache satrecs by satnum and pin one satellite per worker lane (FNV-1a) rather than re-sending TLE records per request; set `ArcType.NONE` and sane `PathGraphics` resolution from day one (a Cesium community thread shows 5 fps at 1 entity purely from bad interpolation granularity, not scale).
- Test oracle: reproduce satvis's own accuracy check — `GridPositionProperty` interpolation error vs python-sgp4 should land in the same regime (single-digit metres for near-circular LEO, tens of metres for eccentric HEO) at whatever stencil degree is chosen; a cubic stencil is demonstrably insufficient (3.7 km error on a 3.5-day HEO).

**Phase A (pass prediction, borrowed from Gpredict/PREDICT/satvis).**
- Implement look angles from ECEF (SEZ rotation: el=arcsin(Z/rho), az=atan2(E,-S) mod 360°) as an early, standalone, testable function — this is the frame-chain test (TEME→GMST→ECEF→topocentric) worth writing before anything else in this area.
- Test oracle: reproduce a pass table for one ground station against a real TLE and check it against `skyfield.find_events` with the same elevation mask — should agree within the sampling step (10 s in the reference reproduction here).
- Pitfalls to encode: PREDICT's west-positive-longitude convention (don't accidentally adopt it); azimuth at the apex of a near-zenith pass is ill-conditioned — report to whole degrees or use compass points; mixed Earth-radius conventions (6371 vs 6378.137 km) between different formulas in the same codebase.
- Run pass prediction in a dedicated worker separate from the position-sampling worker once catalog size grows past a few hundred satellites against one station (satvis's own number: ~8 ms of SGP4 per satellite per station).

**Phase B (heliocentric/interplanetary layer).**
- Do not build on spacekit; roll a custom Three.js scene on a current release (WebGL2, not spacekit's dead WebGL1 fork), but borrow under MIT: the `Ephem`/`EphemerisTable` (Lagrange-interpolated [JD,x,y,z,vx,vy,vz]) ingestion pattern for JPL-quality vectors, the `KeplerParticles`-style GPU-side Kepler solve for any asteroid/debris-scale catalog, the `jdPerSecond` clock API, and `orbitAround` parent-chaining.
- Design requirement, not optional: double-precision JS positions with camera-relative float32 upload (a floating origin), `logarithmicDepthBuffer` or a multi-pass near/far scheme, and a compressed-radius (log or power-law) toggle applied consistently to both bodies and transfer arcs — spacekit's absence of all three is the specific, measured reason it cannot host both Earth-orbit and heliocentric content in one scene (float32 ULP at 1 AU = 17.8 km, 4.5% of a LEO altitude).
- Source planet positions from a JPL-quality table (e.g. `jplephem`/DE440 or Horizons-exported vectors fed through the `EphemerisTable` pattern), not fixed osculating elements — the measured drift (0.38° for Saturn by 2026) is large enough to corrupt any downstream Lambert/porkchop C3/v_infinity computation.
- Borrow NASA Eyes' UX patterns, not its engine: a visible time-rate dial, trajectory arcs with discrete encounter/date markers, an explicit "not to scale" toggle, and one scripted guided tour as the highest-leverage demo feature.

**Phase B/C (fidelity disclosure).**
- Ship an explicit, GMAT-anchored fidelity table in the docs/README (feature | GMAT reference | this tool) covering: finite burns, full force models, B-plane targeting, continuous NLP optimization, navigation uncertainty, physically-coupled eclipses, ground-contact link budgets, mission-level Δv budgets, formation flying, hardware modeling — all confirmed absent from a v1 impulsive-Δv/patched-conic/discrete-graph-search design. This preempts the "did you know your model ignores X" question from a domain-expert reviewer.

**Phase C (Pareto route explorer — the research claim).**
- The gap claim to bring to the professor is the corrected, narrower one from §5/§4-Q3 and from gap_statement.md (which lists four 2026 hobbyist single-objective porkchop-to-3D repos that must be acknowledged): no open-source project found (checked broadly on GitHub, and specifically against ESA's GODOT) couples an interactive 3D/WebGL scene to genuine multi-objective (EMOA*-style) trajectory optimization; GMAT is the closest bridge and is single-objective/targeting only. Do not state the broader "nowhere in open source" version — it is not defensible after the verification pass, and a professor who co-authored EMOA* is likely to know of edge cases (GODOT, ZERUA-class small projects) that would undercut an overclaimed absolute.
- Reuse satvis's pass-timeline + entity-info-panel + shareable-URL-state pattern as the UI substrate: a selected route = a selected entity with tabs, exactly the interaction model satvis already proved works for satellites and passes.
- License due diligence before depending on any astrodynamics library: pykep (MPL-2.0, active) is the safer Lambert/optimization dependency; poliastro (archived) and its fork hapsira (stale, ~2.3 years dormant on its actual development branch) are real risks, not merely "check before using" footnotes.

## 8. Glossary of the credibility vocabulary encountered

- **verified** — read directly from a primary source (source code, a repo's own LICENSE file, a live API response, a reproduced calculation) by the person making the claim, with the artifact quoted or the computation shown.
- **likely** — supported by a secondary source (a search-result snippet, a summary of a page that returned an error on direct fetch, general domain knowledge cross-checked against at least one real source) but not confirmed by opening the primary document directly.
- **uncertain** — inferred from circumstantial evidence (e.g., file presence suggesting a development practice) or from the *absence* of a source after a genuine search, and explicitly flagged as such rather than asserted.
- **self-reported / vendor claim** — a number or capability stated by a project's own maintainers (e.g., KeepTrack's "2.5 million objects at 60 fps") that no reader in this track independently reproduced; treated as a marketing ceiling, not a measured fact, until benchmarked.
- **confirmed / needs_qualification / plausible** (verification-ledger verdicts) — "confirmed" means an independent re-check reproduced the claim as stated or with only cosmetic differences; "needs_qualification" means the substance holds but the precise wording, a specific number, or an unstated caveat was wrong or overstated and has been corrected in this document; the ledger did not produce a flat "refuted" verdict on any of the six audited claims, though several table cells fell under a corrected wording that is materially different from the original phrasing.
- **A/B/A trial vs. a benchmark sweep** — a single manual before/after/before-again measurement (used by satvis's own authors when their normal statistical benchmark harness could not resolve a metric reliably) vs. a repeated, drift-checked, percentile-reported measurement; the former is real evidence but weaker than the latter, and this document now distinguishes them explicitly wherever satvis's own performance numbers are cited.
- **point-in-time snapshot vs. stable census** — a GitHub API/search result reflects the state of the platform at the moment it was queried; for low-star or niche queries, ranking and result sets were shown (via an independent same-day rerun in this track) to shift meaningfully within hours, so any "N repositories found" figure should be treated as a snapshot dated to its fetch time, not a permanent count.
- **closed-source / access-gated vs. open-source** — a distinction this document enforces carefully in §4-Q3 and §5: ESA's GODOT is real, documented, and has genuine capability, but it sits behind institutional SSO/partner registration on a private GitLab instance, which is a materially different reason for its invisibility to a public scan than simply "wrong platform," and it should not be waved away as equivalent to "does not exist."
- **AGPL network-use / copyleft clause** — the specific AGPL provision (absent from plain GPL) that treats running modified software as a network service as equivalent to distribution, meaning a public web app built on AGPL code must release its combined source; this is the operative constraint on ever copying KeepTrack source into this project's MIT codebase.
